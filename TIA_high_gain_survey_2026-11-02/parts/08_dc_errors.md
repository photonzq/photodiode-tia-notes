## 8. DC errors, leakage and guarding

At 100 MΩ and above, Chapter 6 found that the amplifiers differ more in DC accuracy than in noise. This chapter collects the DC errors, the board-level effects that are as large as the amplifier's own errors at those gains, and the dependence of the bias current on the voltage at which the inputs sit.

### 8.1 The DC error budget

The output error of the TIA in the dark has three sources: the offset voltage, multiplied by the low-frequency noise gain; the bias current; and any leakage current into the summing node, both multiplied by $R_F$:

$$
V_{err} = V_{OS}\left(1 + \frac{R_F}{R_{sh}}\right) + \bigl(I_B + I_{leak}\bigr)R_F .
\tag{38}
$$

Analog Devices gives the same terms for a photodiode TIA (ADA4530-1 data sheet, p. 44, Eqs. 17–20) [D1]. Table A.3 evaluates Eq. (38) without leakage, with the maximum $V_{OS}$ and the maximum $I_B$ at 25 °C and 85 °C, for every design. On the small, high-shunt-resistance photodiode the offset dominates up to about 10 MΩ and the bias current above, as Section 6.4 described. On the 14-kΩ photodiode the offset term dominates at every gain, because $R_F/R_{sh}$ reaches 71 at 1 MΩ. The bias current in these tables is the datasheet's, which holds at the input common-mode voltage of its specification, in the circuit of Figure 1 mid-supply; Section 8.6 shows how far it moves elsewhere.

A resistor in series with the non-inverting input, equal to $R_F\parallel R_{sh}$, cancels the bias-current error when the two input currents match, as in bipolar parts. TI's OPA128 photodiode circuit uses 400 MΩ there, bypassed by 1000 pF so that its Johnson noise does not reach the amplifier (SBOA061, p. 2, Fig. 3) [A2]. For FET-input parts the two bias currents do not match. The OPA656 data sheet therefore recommends no resistor at the non-inverting input, because the bias currents "do not match but are so low to begin with (< 20 pA) that dc errors due to input bias currents are negligible" (OPA656 data sheet, p. 14) [D16]. Unbypassed, any resistance $R$ there adds $\sqrt{4k_BTR}$ to $e_n$, which the node capacitance then turns into current noise (the companion's Section 3.10).

### 8.2 Leakage and guarding

A leakage resistance $R_{ins}$ from a conductor at potential $\Delta V$ to the summing node injects $\Delta V/R_{ins}$, which Eq. (38) multiplies by $R_F$. Analog Devices writes that it is "very difficult to maintain high insulation resistance values in glass epoxy (such as FR-4) PCB materials", and that "resistance values of 10 TΩ to 100 TΩ are achievable" (ADA4530-1 data sheet, p. 34) [D1]. With 10 V across 10 TΩ, that is 1 pA, or 1 mV at the output of a 1-GΩ TIA. That is comparable with the bias current of a precision JFET part at 25 °C and fifty times the 20-fA limit of an electrometer. The cure is a guard: a conductor that surrounds the summing node and is held at the summing node's own potential. The insulation between guard and node then sees only the offset voltage, microvolts, and its leakage becomes negligible, while the leakage from other conductors flows into the guard instead of the node. For the inverting TIA with its non-inverting input grounded, the summing node is at ground potential, so the guard is a ground ring around it. For a single-supply TIA with the non-inverting input at a mid-supply voltage, the guard must be driven at that voltage. The integrated guard buffers of ADA4530-1 and OPA928 do exactly this, within 100 µV and 50 µV maximum (Section 5.1). Close to the negative supply rail they can no longer follow the input, and the bias current rises (Section 8.6). Hamamatsu likewise recommends guard rings and PTFE terminals for photodiode amplifiers at high gain (KSPD9001E04, p. 10) [P5].

### 8.3 Dielectric relaxation

Every insulator polarizes with a delay after the voltage across it changes, and the polarization current that follows can last for minutes. Analog Devices measured it on two board laminates with a large alternating test voltage. FR-4 "requires 1 hour to dissipate the dielectric relaxation current to less than 10 fA", while Rogers 4350B, a ceramic-filled laminate, "requires less than 20 sec to dissipate the dielectric relaxation current to less than 1 fA" (ADA4530-1 data sheet, p. 35) [D1]. For a high-gain TIA this matters whenever a voltage near the summing node changes: when the photodiode's bias is switched, after a range change (Section 7.1), or after the output has saturated. A feedback or integration capacitor, if one is added, should likewise have a low-absorption dielectric.

### 8.4 Humidity, contamination and the layout of the summing node

The insulation resistance of the board depends on moisture. Analog Devices writes that at lower temperatures, below about 70 °C, "the insulation resistance creates more significant leakage current errors than the amplifier itself", so that "the relative humidity of the air is the most important error source at lower temperatures" (ADA4530-1 data sheet, p. 35) [D1]. Its bias-current limits are therefore specified at a relative humidity below 50% (p. 3). Flux residues and handling contamination have the same effect, and the same data sheet devotes sections to cleaning and handling, solder paste, guard rings, guard planes and via fences, cables and connectors (pp. 37–43). These were not read in detail for this note (Appendix C, item 7).

Section 3.4 adds three layout rules for the feedback resistor and the summing node.

- **No grounded plane under the feedback resistor.** A grounded plane makes the response peak (Table 3). Either cut the plane away, or replace it under the resistor by copper driven at one third of the output, or by strips at graded potentials, from a resistive divider between the output and ground (Section 3.4, Figure 6, Table 9.4). Copper tied to the output also stops the peaking, but it divides the bandwidth by three to four. Copper around the resistor should never be tied to the summing node itself, which slows the response as much as tying it to the output and adds to the node's capacitance and leakage. The strip nearest the summing node must be separated from it by the summing node's own guard ring.
- **A short summing node.** Keep it with the photodiode close by, because every picofarad there adds to $C_T$ and thus to the $e_n\,\omega C$ noise. A photodiode at the end of a cable adds the cable's capacitance in full, together with the noise that the cable's movement generates.
- **The guard ring of Section 8.2** around the summing node and the non-inverting input, on both sides of the board where possible. Where the ring passes under the feedback resistor, as in the guard layouts of the ADA4530-1 and LMP7721 data sheets, it is the grounded conductor of Section 3.4 under part of the film. It then lowers the resistor's first-order capacitance, makes the resistor a distributed line at its corner and raises its noise there by 1.0–1.8 dB, as the field solutions summarized in Section 3.4 show (the side note's Section 7).
- **A shielded enclosure for the whole front end.** The photodiode, the summing node, the feedback network and the amplifier belong together in a closed, grounded metal enclosure, with any cable to the photodiode shielded as well. At 1 GΩ, 1 fF of stray capacitance to a 1-V, 50-Hz source already gives 0.31 mV at the output (Section 3.4). Copper around the feedback resistor does not replace this enclosure; it only sets the resistor's surroundings inside it.

### 8.5 Temperature

Section 2.7 showed that the bias current of a FET input doubles every 10 °C (Eq. (15)) and that the photodiode's shunt resistance halves every 5–10 °C, while the resistor's noise rises only as $\sqrt{T}$. Both effects enter Eq. (38): the bias-current term grows by 64 from 25 °C to 85 °C, and the offset term by the growth of $R_F/R_{sh}$. For the S1336-18BK at 1 GΩ, $R_F/R_{sh}$ rises from 0.5 at 25 °C to about 2200 at 85 °C, using the dark-current coefficient as in Section 2.7, so that 25 µV of offset becomes a 55-mV error. Weak-light measurements at high gain therefore need the photodiode, and preferably the whole summing node, at a controlled temperature, and a cooled photodiode where the shunt resistance limits the floor (Section 6.7).

### 8.6 The input common-mode voltage and the bias current

The bias current that Eq. (38) multiplies by $R_F$, and whose shot noise adds to the current noise, is a datasheet value taken at one input common-mode voltage. In the circuit of Figure 1 the non-inverting input is grounded between split supplies, so both inputs sit at mid-supply, and that is where most datasheets specify the bias current. A single-supply circuit holds the non-inverting input at a reference voltage $V_{REF}$ instead, and so does a circuit that moves it to bias the photodiode or to gain output swing. The inputs then sit at $V_{REF}$, and for several amplifiers the bias current there is ten to several hundred times larger, although $V_{REF}$ still lies inside the guaranteed input range. Table A.9 collects, for all 25 op amps, the input range, the common-mode voltage of the bias-current specification and what the datasheets' curves show, and Figure A.1 draws the same limits. Below, $V_-$ and $V_+$ are the negative and positive supply rails.

**What the datasheets specify.** Most datasheets give the bias current at mid-supply. Five of the parts give it elsewhere: OPA928 at $V_+ - 3$ V in its table for 4.5–8-V supplies (§5.5, p. 4) [D2], LMP7721 at 1 V on 2.5-V and 5-V supplies (§6.5 and §6.6, pp. 5–6) [D3], LMC662 at 1.5 V on 5 V (§5.6, p. 6) [D5], ADA4625-1 at 1.5 V on 5 V, where mid-supply lies outside its input range (p. 5, Table 3) [D6], and LTC6268-10 at 2.75 V and 4.0 V on 5 V (5.0V Electrical Characteristics table, p. 3) [D18]. Here § marks a numbered section of a datasheet, which is how TI numbers its specification tables. For seven parts the input range excludes the negative rail. OPA656, OPA657, OPA855 and OPA955 stop 0.7–1.5 V above it, and OPA828, OPA827 and OPA211 1.8–3 V above it. OPA818 and OPA858 reach it only as a typical value; their guaranteed ranges begin at $V_- + 0.25$ V and $V_- + 0.4$ V (Table A.9).

**Bootstrapped and guarded inputs.** LTC6268-10 shows the effect most clearly. Its bias current is ±3 fA typical and ±20 fA maximum at 2.75 V and 4.0 V on 5 V, and its input range, guaranteed by the test of its common-mode rejection, runs from −0.1 V to 4.5 V (5.0V Electrical Characteristics table, p. 3) [D18]. Its datasheet curve stays within ±10 fA only from 0.55 V to 4.5 V. It reaches −100 fA at 0.15 V and −260 to −300 fA at 0 V, about a hundred times its mid-supply value and still inside the guaranteed range (curve G10, p. 8) [D18]. Brisebois shows the same curve, measured on the unity-gain LTC6268 with a 1-TΩ sense resistor wired in air to the input pin over a hole cut in the board ([A12], Figs. 12–14). The datasheet gives the reason: each input is protected by two diodes in series to each rail, and the node between them is driven by a buffered copy of the input, so that the diodes on the pin carry almost no voltage (Applications Information, ESD, p. 17) [D18]. Near a rail the buffer can no longer follow the input, the diodes take up the difference, and their leakage reaches the pin.

The guarded electrometers rely on the same principle and share its limit. ADA4530-1 connects each input to its guard through antiparallel diodes, which the guard buffer holds at zero volts (Theory of Operation, ESD Structure, p. 28) [D1]. The buffer's output reaches down only to 0.1 V above $V_-$ (p. 5, Table 2), and the datasheet therefore advises against a common-mode voltage within 100 mV of $V_-$ (Theory of Operation, Guard Buffer, p. 29) [D1]. Its curves, given only at 85 °C and 125 °C, show why. At 125 °C and 0.1 V several units carry three to four times their mid-supply current, and some more than ten times, beyond −600 fA against a limit of ±250 fA (Figs. 29, 32 and 33, p. 15) [D1]. The datasheet marks $V_- + 1.5$ V to $V_+ - 1.5$ V as the preferred range (Figs. 22–33, pp. 14–15) [D1]. For OPA928 at the negative rail the guard pins decide. At 85 °C its bias current stays at −1.5 fA down to $V_-$ when the guard pins are grounded, but falls below −20 fA within about 50 mV of $V_-$ when its internal buffer drives them (Fig. 7-5, p. 30) [D2]. At 25 °C it stays within ±1 fA from $V_-$ to $V_+ - 0.75$ V on 4.5-V and 16-V supplies (Figs. 5-23 and 5-25, pp. 13–14) [D2]. On 36 V it rises above $V_- + 20$ V, to 880 fA at $V_+ - 2.5$ V (Fig. 5-27, p. 14) [D2], which is why its 75-fA limit for supplies above 16 V holds only below $V_- + 20$ V (§5.7, note 1, p. 9; §6.3.6, p. 26).

**Other inputs.** Without such a buffer the bias current can rise as steeply near a rail.

- **LMC662** reads about −1 pA at $V_-$ on ±2.5 V, against less than 0.01 pA from $V_- + 0.45$ V up to the top of its range, and on ±7.5 V it reads +0.35 pA at that top, $V_+ - 2.3$ V (Figs. 5-6 and 5-5, p. 10) [D5]. Against its 2-fA typical (§5.6, p. 6) that is 500 and 175 times.
- **LMC6001A** exceeds 25 fA within 0.08 V of $V_-$, against 6.1 fA at mid-supply (Fig. 2, p. 11) [D4].
- **OPA2156** passes −100 pA at $V_-$ and +100 pA at $V_+ + 0.1$ V, the two ends of its range, against +12.3 and +6.5 pA at mid-supply (Fig. 12, p. 9) [D12].
- **OPA320** reads +1.9 pA at $V_- + 0.05$ V and −5.0 pA at $V_+ - 0.05$ V, against +0.22 pA at mid-supply (Fig. 8, p. 11) and a maximum of 0.9 pA (§6.7, p. 7) [D13].
- **LMP7721** leaves its ±50-fA scale above 3.5 V on 5 V, inside its range, which runs to 4 V, against −2.1 fA at 2.5 V (Fig. 2, p. 8) [D3].
- **OPA388** stays within its ±25-pA ripple up to about 1 V below $V_+$ and then reaches about 720 pA at the top of its range (Fig. 6-10, p. 12), 24 times its 30-pA typical (§6.7, p. 8) [D15].
- **OPA858** reads −3.2 pA at 3.4 V, its guaranteed top on 5 V, and −9.5 pA at 3.6 V, its typical top, against −1.3 pA at mid-supply (Fig. 6-39, p. 14) [D32].
- **ADA4625-1** on ±18 V stays within ±4 pA of its mid-supply value from −16.5 V to +11.5 V on all five units plotted, but two of them pass ±100 pA at 14.1–14.2 V, inside its range, which ends at 14.5 V (Fig. 16, p. 11) [D6].
- **OPA828** passes ±30 pA within 0.2 V of both ends of its range, against 1.1–1.2 pA at mid-supply (Fig. 6-14, p. 10) [D7].

OPA656, OPA657, OPA855, OPA140, OPA145, OPA827, OPA1656 and OPA211 stay within about twice their mid-supply values across their ranges, and OPA381 below 7.3 pA (Table A.9). ADA4817-1, OPA955 and LMH6629 give no curve, so their bias current away from mid-supply is not known (Appendix C, item 24).

**Temperature.** The dependence grows in absolute terms with temperature, as Section 8.5 leads one to expect. At 85 °C OPA818 carries 767 pA at $V_-$ against 347 pA at mid-supply (Fig. 6-30, p. 11), above its 700-pA limit (§6.5, p. 5) [D20]. At 125 °C LMP7721 reads +7.1 pA at 0 V (Fig. 5, p. 8), beyond its ±5-pA limit (§6.6, p. 6) [D3], and the one ADA4625-1 unit plotted reads 5.85 nA at $V_-$ on ±18 V against 2.69 nA at mid-supply (Fig. 17, p. 11), above the 5.5-nA limit of Table A.1 (p. 3, Table 2) [D6].

**What it costs.** The DC error of Eq. (38) grows by the change of the bias current times $R_F$. For LMC662 at 1 GΩ and 25 °C, moving the inputs from mid-supply to $V_-$ raises the bias-current term from $2\ \mathrm{fA}\times1\ \mathrm{G\Omega} = 2\ \mu\mathrm{V}$ to about $1\ \mathrm{pA}\times1\ \mathrm{G\Omega} = 1\ \mathrm{mV}$. For OPA818 at 100 MΩ and 85 °C it raises it from $347\ \mathrm{pA}\times100\ \mathrm{M\Omega} = 34.7\ \mathrm{mV}$ to $767\ \mathrm{pA}\times100\ \mathrm{M\Omega} = 76.7\ \mathrm{mV}$. If the extra current is junction leakage, as the datasheets' explanations suggest, it also carries full shot noise. Its power density is then at least $2q\lvert I_B\rvert$, even where the currents of two junctions partly cancel, because their noise powers add. That equals the Johnson noise of $R_F$ at the current $I_x = 51.4\ \mathrm{mV}/R_F$ of Eq. (2), 51.4 pA at 1 GΩ. OPA828 at 1 GΩ with its inputs within 0.2 V of a limit, where its bias current exceeds 30 pA, would therefore add at least $2q\times30\ \mathrm{pA} = 9.6\times10^{-30}\ \mathrm{A^2/Hz}$ to the resistor's $4k_BT/R_F = 1.65\times10^{-29}\ \mathrm{A^2/Hz}$, or 2.0 dB of noise. The femtoampere currents of the electrometers and of LTC6268-10 stay far below that level even near the rails.

**Choosing the reference voltage.** The reference voltage sets three things at once: the common-mode voltage of the inputs, the photodiode's bias and the dark level of the output. With the photodiode's anode on the summing node and its cathode at the bias voltage $V_B$, as in Figure 1, the photocurrent flows into the summing node, and

$$
V_R = V_B - V_{REF},\qquad V_{out} = V_{REF} - I_{ph}R_F ,
\tag{39}
$$

where $V_R$ is the photodiode's reverse bias. The output falls from $V_{REF}$ as the light rises, so the swing $V_{sw}$ of Eq. (3) is the distance from $V_{REF}$ down to the lowest output voltage. Raising $V_{REF}$ buys swing but moves the inputs toward $V_+$. Four rules follow from Table A.9.

- **Split supplies with the non-inverting input grounded** put the inputs at mid-supply, where most bias currents are specified. That is the circuit of Figure 1, and of Brisebois's TIA, which runs LTC6268-10 on ±2.5 V with the photodiode's cathode at +2.5 V ([A12], Fig. 3).
- **On a single supply at zero bias**, the photodiode's cathode returns to $V_{REF}$ rather than to ground, so that $V_R = 0$, and $V_{REF}$ sits near mid-supply. A grounded non-inverting input would put LTC6268-10 at about a hundred times its mid-supply bias current and LMC662 at five hundred times its typical, rule out seven parts and leave OPA818 and OPA858 on a typical value.
- **On a single supply with reverse bias**, the cathode returns to $V_+$, so that $V_R = V_+ - V_{REF}$, and $V_{REF}$ rises for swing only as far as the bias current stays near its mid-supply value: to 4.5 V for LTC6268-10 and to about 3.3 V for OPA858 on 5 V. TI's own avalanche-photodiode circuit holds the non-inverting input of OPA858 at 3.4 V (Fig. 9-1, p. 21) [D32], where its bias current is 2.4 times the mid-supply value.
- **The guarded electrometers** need their inputs at least 0.1 V, and preferably 1.5 V, above $V_-$ (ADA4530-1), or their guard pins grounded when the inputs sit at $V_-$ (OPA928).

The model of this note uses the bias currents of Table A.1, which hold at the common-mode voltages of Table A.9, and so in practice for the circuit of Figure 1. For any other reference voltage, the bias current in Eq. (38) and in the noise is the one that the datasheet's curve gives at that voltage and temperature.
